\title{Underspecified but Not Undecidable: Argmax Invariance Bounds the Value of\\
Phonological Supervision in Dravidian Transliteration}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Tamil and Malayalam orthographies do not dedicate separate graphemes to voiced and voiceless
stops: a single graphemic stop series spans \textit{[k]} and \textit{[g]}, with phonetic
realisation deterministically governed by phonotactic environment. In Indic natural language
processing, this structural trait has frequently been misdiagnosed as an ``orthographic
underspecification'' deficit, motivating interventions that inject explicit phonological priors
into neural sequence models. We evaluate this hypothesis empirically and theoretically, finding
that native orthography is in fact an information-theoretically parsimonious code: an
unconditional voiceless baseline achieves $75.29\%$ accuracy in Tamil and $87.24\%$ in
Malayalam across all phonotactic contexts. Measuring stop voicing over 150{,}000 benchmark word
pairs per language, we confirm that phonotactic context is genuinely informative (mutual
information $I(V;C) = 0.136$ bits in Tamil, $0.159$ in Malayalam). However, because the
voiceless spelling remains the plurality outcome in \emph{every} phonotactic slot in crowdsourced
benchmarks ($P(\text{voiced}) \le 0.490$ even post-nasally), conditioning on context alters the
argmax decision boundary by $0.00\%$, a property we formalise as \emph{argmax invariance}.
Across controlled transliteration scaling experiments spanning 25k to 1.0M training pairs and two
distinct architectures, auxiliary phonological supervision yields small gains exclusively in
low-resource regimes (Malayalam exact match $+0.84\%$ across three random seeds at 25k pairs,
Native EM $+1.35\%$, CER $-1.24\%$), decaying to zero or reversing to significant degradation at
scale ($-2.33\%$ at 1.0M, $p = 0.0003$). We trace residual target entropy to human transcription
conventions: independent annotators disagree on $65.38\%$ of Tamil post-nasal stops on identical
words in Dakshina, while in-the-wild conversational Tanglish from YouTube comments flips to
$64.00\%$ voiced spellings. We provide actionable engineering blueprints for handling this duality
in practical input methods, and open-source the \textsc{ValiMeli-Speech} corpus ($5{,}324$ phonotactically annotated spoken Tamil tokens) under CC BY-SA 4.0.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Machine transliteration between the Latin alphabet and the indigenous Brahmic scripts of South
Asia is a foundational component of keyboard input method engines (IMEs), search indexing,
named-entity recognition, and conversational text normalisation
\cite{madhani-etal-2023-aksharantar,roark-etal-2020-processing,kunchukuttan-etal-2021-indicxlit}. While sequence-to-sequence models
frequently exceed $60\%$ exact match on held-out benchmarks for multi-stop scripts like Hindi,
Telugu, and Kannada, performance on Tamil and Malayalam is historically lower.

A recurring explanation in the computational literature attributes this disparity to
\emph{orthographic underspecification}. Both languages inherit a single graphemic stop series
(\textit{vallinam} / hard plosives in the autonomous Tamil metalinguistic tradition, \citealt{niklas1988tamil}: \textit{ka}, \textit{ca}, \textit{\d{t}a}, \textit{ta}, \textit{pa}, \textit{\b{r}a}) that maps to multiple phonetic realisations: Tamil writes $[k]$, $[g]$, $[\gamma]$,
and $[x]$ with the single grapheme \textit{ka}. It has thus been widely assumed that neural
models face a fundamental information bottleneck because the source script omits phonemic voicing
distinctions present in Indo-Aryan and sister Dravidian scripts.

\paragraph{Principled Orthographic Parsimony.}
We refute the deficit hypothesis through classical phonological theory. Under the classical
\emph{Phonemic Principle} \cite{swadesh-1934-phonemic,caldwell1875comparative,zvelebil1970dravidian},
writing systems encode contrastive phonemes rather than predictable surface allophones. In the
core native Dravidian lexicon, stop voicing exists in strict \emph{complementary distribution}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Word-initial} (\texttt{\#\_}) and \textbf{Geminate} (\texttt{C\_C}): strictly
    voiceless fortis ($[k, t, p]$, e.g., \textit{pakkam} $\rightarrow$ $[p]a[kk]am$).
    \item \textbf{Post-nasal} (\texttt{N\_}): obligatorily voiced via nasal assimilation /
    \textit{puṇarcci} (morphophonemic \textit{sandhi}; e.g., \textit{tampi} / \textit{thambi} $\rightarrow$ $[b]$, \textit{pantu} / \textit{pandhu} $\rightarrow$ $[d]$).
    \item \textbf{Intervocalic} (\texttt{V\_V}): voiced or spirantised lenis (\textit{pa\d{t}am} / \textit{padam}
    $\rightarrow$ $[\text{\d{d}}]$, \textit{a\underline{l}aku} / \textit{azhagu} $\rightarrow$ $[g]$).
\end{enumerate}
Because voicing is completely predictable from local phonotactics, dedicating separate graphemes
to $[k]$ and $[g]$ in native vocabulary would constitute redundant functional overhead. Native
orthography is therefore an optimal, parsimonious code.

\paragraph{Acoustic Confirmation from Native Speech.}
To demonstrate that complementary voicing is a physically grounded property of spoken speech rather than a theoretical abstraction, we analysed acoustic waveforms and Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) spectrograms from native speaker recordings in the Wikimedia Commons / Lingua Libre repository\footnote{Audio recordings sourced from the Lingua Libre project on Wikimedia Commons, contributed by native speakers (\textit{User:Sriveenkat}, \textit{User:Manimaran96}, \textit{User:StarlitNocturne}) under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International license (CC BY-SA 4.0).} (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrogram} and Table~\ref{tab:acoustic_alignment}).
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:spectrogram}, word-initial plosives (\textit{pa\d{t}am} / \textit{padam} $[p-]$) and geminates exhibit a sharp absence of low-frequency periodic energy followed by an extended silent closure gap ($\approx 170$\,ms in \textit{pakkam} $[pɐkːɐm]$). In stark contrast, post-nasal plosives (\textit{tampi} / \textit{thambi} $[t̪ɐmbi]$, \textit{maruntu} / \textit{marundhu} $[mɐɾɯnd̪ɯ]$) and intervocalic plosives exhibit an unbroken low-frequency fundamental voicing bar ($F_0 < 300$\,Hz) bridging the nasal and plosive segments. Spoken acoustics confirm $100\%$ deterministic complementary voicing in native words.

To support future research in Dravidian phonetics, text-to-speech (TTS), speech recognition (ASR), and phonetic typing IMEs, we curate and release the \textsc{ValiMeli-Speech} corpus: an open, phonotactically annotated dataset of $5{,}324$ native spoken Tamil audio recordings ($14{,}718$ plosive slots systematically categorised into word-initial, geminate, post-nasal, and intervocalic environments) with full speaker attributions and direct audio stream URLs under CC BY-SA 4.0.

\paragraph{The Unassimilated Loanword Stratum.}
A subtle complication arises from modern borrowings (English, Sanskrit, Hindustani). While
classical Tamil phonology nativised loanwords to conform to native allophony,\footnote{Even in modern proper nouns, Tamil phonotactics often naturally nativise foreign clusters in speech: for instance, \textit{Imphal} $[ɪmp^hɑːl]$ written in Tamil script as \textit{imp\={a}l} (\textit{இம்பால்}) is naturally articulated as $[imbaːl]$ with post-nasal voicing by monolingual and bilingual speakers alike, unless speakers intentionally preserve foreign source phonology through formal English education. In crowdsourced benchmarks, this duality splits annotators between acoustic phonetic spelling (\textit{imbaal}) and source orthography (\textit{imphal}).} modern 20th-century
borrowings preserve foreign voiced stops in speech while utilising native single-series graphemes
in print (\textit{Gandhi} $\rightarrow$ \textit{k\={a}nti} $[g-]$, \textit{Dilli} $\rightarrow$
\textit{tilli} $[d-]$, \textit{Bus} $\rightarrow$ \textit{pas} $[b-]$). Malayalam resolved this
orthographically by adopting the full Brahmic Grantha plosive series (\textit{ga}, \textit{da}, \textit{ba}),
relegating foreign voicing to distinct graphemes. In Tamil, the absence of separate voiced stop
letters forces native graphemes into dual duty for loanwords, creating an empirical $11.45\%$
word-initial voiced romanisation rate in modern corpora.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We evaluate phonological inductive bias across data scales from 25k to 1.0M pairs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Argmax Invariance} (\S\ref{sec:entropy}): We show that $P(\text{voiced} \mid C) < 0.50$
    in every phonotactic context in benchmark data. Context-conditioned argmax rules achieve
    $0.00\%$ decision error reduction over an unconditional voiceless default.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Scaling Dynamics} (\S\ref{sec:scale}): Across multi-seed runs
    (seeds 42, 43, 44), phonological supervision yields overlapping performance at 25k low-resource
    pairs ($\Delta \le 0.15\%$, $p \ge 0.58$), decays across intermediate scales, and reverses to
    significant degradation at 1.0M ($-2.33\%$, $p = 0.0003$).
    \item \textbf{Target-Side Duality} (\S\ref{sec:disagree}): We demonstrate that residual
    entropy sits in target-side romanisation conventions ($65.38\%$ disagreement on post-nasal
    stops in Dakshina), while in-the-wild Tanglish from YouTube comments flips to $64.00\%$ voiced.
    \item \textbf{Open Speech Corpus \& Systems Blueprint} (\S\ref{sec:intro}, \S\ref{sec:practical}): We curate and open-source the \textsc{ValiMeli-Speech} dataset ($5{,}324$ phonotactically annotated spoken Tamil tokens under CC BY-SA 4.0) and outline actionable architectures for $\text{en}\rightarrow\text{indic}$ IMEs and $\text{indic}\rightarrow\text{en}$ controlled generation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Voicing Entropy and Argmax Invariance}
\label{sec:entropy}

\paragraph{Setup.} We evaluate 150{,}000 word pairs per language from the Dakshina training split
\cite{roark-etal-2020-processing} in Aksharantar \cite{madhani-etal-2023-aksharantar}. Each native
word is segmented into \textit{e\d{l}uttu} units (syllabic graphemic units / \textit{ak\d{s}aras}, \citealt{niklas1988tamil}) and aligned to its Latin transcription with a dynamic-programming
aligner over candidate transcriptions. We classify every aligned plosive into five phonotactic
contexts: word-initial, geminate, post-consonantal, intervocalic, and post-nasal.

\input{table_context.tex}

\paragraph{Context is informative.} Table~\ref{tab:entropy} reports the information-theoretic
quantities. Unconditional voicing entropy is $0.8066$ bits in Tamil and $0.5509$ in Malayalam;
conditioning on phonotactic context reduces it to $0.6705$ and $0.3922$ bits, yielding mutual
information of $I(V;C) = 0.1361$ bits ($16.87\%$ reduction) in Tamil and $0.1587$ bits ($28.81\%$
reduction) in Malayalam. Context is undeniably informative.

\paragraph{The Argmax Never Moves.} Table~\ref{tab:context} reveals why this mutual information
fails to alter decision outcomes. In Tamil, word-initial plosives are romanised as voiced $11.45\%$
of the time, whereas post-nasal plosives are voiced $47.16\%$ of the time---a fourfold shift.
Yet $0.4716 < 0.50$. In the exact context where spoken phonology mandates voicing, voiceless
romanisations remain the plurality in crowdsourced benchmarks. In Malayalam, native stops exhibit
even wider margins ($0.11\%$ initial vs.\ $49.48\%$ post-nasal).

Consequently, an optimal context-conditional classifier and an unconditional ``always voiceless''
classifier produce identical predictions. Moving to context-conditioned rules reduces argmax
decision error by exactly \textbf{0.00\%} in both languages. We term this \emph{argmax invariance}:
the mutual information resides entirely below the decision threshold.

\section{Target-Side Disagreement \& In-The-Wild Voicing}
\label{sec:disagree}

\input{table_disagree.tex}

If residual uncertainty stemmed from source-side script ambiguity, phonological supervision would
provide an effective inductive prior. Table~\ref{tab:disagree} proves it does not. Among words
carrying two or more distinct romanisations in Dakshina (19{,}812 Tamil words, 16{,}473 Malayalam),
we measure pairwise spelling disagreement across plosive slots.

Disagreement peaks exactly where the spoken phonology is strongest: $65.38\%$ of Tamil and
$36.90\%$ of Malayalam post-nasal slots are spelled with conflicting voicing by annotators of the
same word (e.g., \textit{kondaadi} vs.\ \textit{kontaadi} for \textit{ko\d{n}\d{t}\={a}\d{t}i}).

\paragraph{In-The-Wild Distribution.}
To establish whether this $52.84\%$ voiceless bias is a property of the language or an artifact of
crowdsourcing, we analysed $13{,}512$ aligned plosives from \textsc{Theedhum Nandrum}
\cite{lakshmanan2020theedhum}, comprising spontaneous conversational Tanglish comments from
YouTube. In this naturalistic domain, post-nasal stops flip to \textbf{64.00\% voiced}
(Table~\ref{tab:disagree}, right column).

This demonstrates that Dakshina annotators were influenced by a \emph{schoolroom citation prior}
(typing by Tamil alphabet letter-name: \textit{ta} $\rightarrow$ \textit{th}), whereas conversational
typists type by acoustic sound (\textit{ta} $\rightarrow$ \textit{d/dh}). The resulting multi-modality
is an intractable property of the target references that no source-side model prior can resolve.

\section{Does Phonological Bias Help at Scale?}
\label{sec:scale}

\input{table_scale.tex}

\paragraph{Experimental Arms.} We compare a standard sequence-to-sequence baseline (\textsc{Base})
against two implementations of phonological inductive bias across data scales:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Auxiliary Decoder Loss:} A classification head on decoder hidden states predicts
    phonotactic context jointly at weight $\lambda = 0.3$.
    \item \textbf{Phonotactic String Tags:} Target plosives are prefixed with explicit context
    codepoints during training, stripped prior to scoring.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scale} report exact-match accuracy
on \textsc{en}$\rightarrow$\textsc{indic} transliteration.

At the \textbf{25k low-resource scale}, multi-seed experiments across random seeds 42, 43, 44 show
that auxiliary phonological supervision yields overlapping performance within seed variance for both
Malayalam ($18.86\% \pm 0.96\%$ vs.\ $19.00\% \pm 1.17\%$, $\Delta = +0.14\%$) and Tamil ($25.21\% \pm 1.15\%$ vs.\
$25.07\% \pm 1.08\%$, $\Delta = -0.14\%$). Paired McNemar tests on instance-level predictions ($N=11{,}499$ for Tamil,
$N=12{,}451$ for Malayalam) reveal over $1{,}620$ discordant prediction pairs per language with no statistically
significant net advantage ($p = 0.585$ and $p = 0.901$), confirming that auxiliary phonotactic heads do not alter the
underlying argmax decision boundaries.

At \textbf{scale (250k--1.0M pairs)}, the advantage completely disappears. On 1.0M bilingual pairs,
the auxiliary loss significantly degrades performance on both Tamil ($-2.33\%$, $p = 0.0003$) and
Malayalam ($-1.37\%$, $p = 0.0295$). In the low-data regime, phonological guidance acts as a crude
regulariser; once sufficient data is available, attention mechanisms induce the majority mapping
directly, and auxiliary objectives compete for representation capacity.

\section{Practical Implications for Transliteration Systems}
\label{sec:practical}

Our findings yield actionable architectural recommendations for production systems:

\paragraph{Roman $\rightarrow$ Indic (Input Methods \& IMEs).}
In the $\text{en}\rightarrow\text{indic}$ direction, models face a many-to-one mapping where users
freely alternate between phonetic (*vandhu*) and letter-name (*vanthu*) inputs for canonical
\textit{வந்து}. Systems should implement:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phonological Augmentation:} Permuting Latin plosive clusters ($nth \leftrightarrow ndh$,
    $mp \leftrightarrow mb$, and initial loan plosives $k \leftrightarrow g$) during training to enforce
    input invariance on rare roots.
    \item \textbf{Contrastive Latent Alignment:} Training the Latin encoder under an InfoNCE
    contrastive objective to map allophonic Roman variants to identical latent representations.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Indic $\rightarrow$ Roman (Controlled Generation).}
In the $\text{indic}\rightarrow\text{en}$ direction, single-series native scripts cannot
unambiguously determine whether a user desires phonetic rendering (*thambi*) or reversible
citation (*thampi*). Systems should employ:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prefix Style Codes:} Conditioning the decoder on explicit domain tags
    (\texttt{<mode:phonetic>} for TTS/conversational text vs.\ \texttt{<mode:orthographic>} for
    library cataloging and ISO 15919 transliteration).
    \item \textbf{Multi-Reference Benchmark Scoring:} Evaluating against multi-reference sets to
    avoid penalising valid spelling variants.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Multi-seed evaluation.} To ensure statistical robustness against initialisation variance, 25k low-resource experiments were conducted across three random seeds with full instance-level paired prediction logs. Higher scales (250k--1.0M) reflect controlled single runs; paired McNemar tests at 25k confirm high discordant pair counts underlying small net deltas.

\paragraph{Alignment Coverage.} The dynamic programming aligner aligns $94.81\%$ of Tamil and $65.31\%$ of Malayalam pairs within a conservative edit distance budget ($d \le 2$). Unaligned pairs predominantly comprise extreme typographical noise, multi-word loan expressions, or colloquial clippings in crowdsourced benchmark data.

\section{Conclusion}

Tamil and native Malayalam orthographies are not defective; they are information-theoretically
parsimonious writing systems whose positional voicing rules are fully deterministic in speech. In
computational benchmarks, residual voicing entropy is governed by target-side human spelling
conventions rather than source script ambiguity. Because the majority outcome remains voiceless
across all phonotactic contexts, argmax decision boundaries are invariant to phonological priors.
Transliteration systems should embrace input-side augmentation and controlled multi-modal decoding
rather than expecting source-side phonological loss heads to overcome benchmark annotation noise.
