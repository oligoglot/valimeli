\title{Underspecified but Not Undecidable: Argmax Invariance Bounds the Value of\\
Phonological Supervision in Dravidian Transliteration}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Tamil and Malayalam orthography does not mark stop voicing: a single grapheme spans
\textit{[k]} and \textit{[g]}, with the realisation determined by phonotactic context.
This has made the two scripts a standing candidate explanation for why multilingual
transliteration systems underperform on them, and a standing motivation for giving models
explicit phonological structure. We test that motivation directly and find it holds only
where data is scarce. Measuring stop voicing over 150{,}000 Dakshina word pairs per language
with a dynamic-programming akshara aligner, we confirm that context is genuinely informative:
mutual information between context and voicing is $0.136$ bits in Tamil and $0.160$ bits in
Malayalam, reducing conditional entropy by $16.9\%$ and $29.1\%$ respectively. Yet the
voiceless realisation is the majority outcome in \emph{every} phonotactic context we
measure---even post-nasally, where sandhi makes voicing obligatory in speech, $P(\text{voiced})$
reaches only $0.472$ in Tamil. Because context never moves the majority outcome, replacing an
unconditional voiceless default with context-conditional rules reduces argmax error by
$0.00\%$ in both languages. We call this \emph{argmax invariance}, and it predicts what we
observe across eight controlled transliteration arms spanning 25k to 1.0M training pairs and
two independent implementations of phonological bias: a significant gain for Malayalam at 25k
pairs ($+1.26$ points, $p=0.009$) that reverses to a significant loss by 1.0M
($-1.37$, $p=0.030$). The residual uncertainty that a phonology-aware model might have
resolved turns out to sit on the target side instead: among Tamil words with more than one
attested romanisation, $65.4\%$ of post-nasal stop slots are spelled inconsistently by
annotators of the same word. Phonological structure is not absent from these scripts; it is
already priced into the majority mapping, and what remains is annotation variance that no
source-side prior can reach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Transliteration between the Latin script and the Brahmic scripts of South Asia is a
character-level sequence transduction task, and for most Indian languages it is close to
solved: recent multilingual models exceed $60\%$ exact match on held-out Dakshina words for
Hindi, Telugu and Kannada \cite{madhani-etal-2023-aksharantar}. Tamil and Malayalam are
consistently harder. A recurring explanation attributes this to orthographic
underspecification. Both scripts inherit a Brahmic plosive series that lost its voicing
contrast: Tamil writes \textit{[k]}, \textit{[g]} and \textit{[G]} with the single
grapheme \textit{ka}, and Malayalam, though it retains distinct voiced letters, uses the
voiceless series for most native vocabulary where voicing is contextually predictable. A
romaniser must therefore recover information the source does not encode.

The natural remedy is to supply that information explicitly---to tag the phonotactic context
of each plosive, or to train the model to predict it, rather than hoping it is induced from
data. This is the phonological analogue of the morphology-aware tokenisation that
\citet{vemula-etal-2025-rethinking} report as beneficial for Indic sequence tasks, and, since
voicing in Dravidian is more directly a function of local phonotactics than morphology is
\cite{lakshmanan2025biconsonantal}, it is the intervention one would expect to help more.

We find that it does not, and the reason is measurable in the corpus before any model is
trained. Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Argmax invariance} (\S\ref{sec:entropy}). Phonotactic context carries real
information about voicing, but it never changes which realisation is most probable. Every
context we measure has $P(\text{voiced})<0.5$. A model minimising expected character error
therefore has nothing to gain from context, and we quantify that as a $0.00\%$ reduction in
argmax error for both languages.
\item \textbf{The advantage is confined to the low-data regime}
(\S\ref{sec:scale}). Across eight controlled arms and two distinct implementations of
phonological bias---an auxiliary decoder loss and phonotactic tags in the output string---the
benefit present at 25k--250k pairs is absent by 500k and significantly negative at 1.0M.
\item \textbf{The residual uncertainty is on the target side}
(\S\ref{sec:disagree}). Among words with multiple attested romanisations, annotators disagree
about voicing on $65.4\%$ of Tamil and $36.9\%$ of Malayalam post-nasal stop slots. This is
noise in the reference, not ambiguity in the source, and no source-side prior can remove it.
\end{enumerate}

We read this as a negative result with a mechanism rather than a failure of the idea. The
phonological analysis that motivates the intervention is correct about the language; it is
simply already implicit in the majority mapping that cross-entropy training recovers on its
own once there is enough data.

\section{Voicing entropy and argmax invariance}
\label{sec:entropy}

\paragraph{Setup.} We take the first 150{,}000 word pairs per language from the Dakshina
training split \cite{roark-etal-2020-processing} as distributed in Aksharantar
\cite{madhani-etal-2023-aksharantar}, segment each native word into aksharas, and align it to
its romanisation with a dynamic-programming aligner over per-akshara candidate transcriptions
(edit budget 2). Alignment succeeds for $94.8\%$ of Tamil and $67.6\%$ of Malayalam words;
the gap reflects Malayalam's richer conjunct inventory, which produces akshara sequences our
candidate generator covers less well. For every aligned plosive we record whether the
romanisation used a voiced or voiceless Latin letter, and label its phonotactic context as
word-initial, geminate, post-consonantal, intervocalic or post-nasal.

\paragraph{Context is informative.} Table~\ref{tab:entropy} reports the result.
Unconditional voicing entropy is $0.807$ bits in Tamil and $0.550$ in Malayalam; conditioning
on context reduces it to $0.671$ and $0.390$, a mutual information of $0.136$ and $0.160$
bits. Phonotactic context is not noise---it predicts voicing about as well as the linguistic
description says it should.

\paragraph{But the argmax never moves.} Table~\ref{tab:context} shows why this does not
translate into any decision-theoretic benefit. The contexts differ enormously in their
voicing rates: Tamil word-initial plosives are romanised with a voiced letter $11.5\%$ of the
time, post-nasal plosives $47.2\%$ of the time---a fourfold difference, and the source of the
mutual information above. But $0.472<0.5$. In the single context where Tamil grammar makes
voicing obligatory, the voiceless spelling is still the plurality choice, and in every other
context it is the overwhelming one. The same holds in Malayalam with wider margins.

The consequence is immediate. A classifier that always predicts ``voiceless'' and a
classifier that applies the optimal context-conditional rule make \emph{identical}
predictions, because the conditional argmax equals the unconditional argmax in all five
contexts. Measured on held-out plosives, moving from the unconditional default to
context-conditional rules reduces error by $0.00\%$ in both languages (Table~\ref{tab:entropy},
final column). We call this \emph{argmax invariance}: the mutual information is real but
lies entirely below the decision threshold.

This is a claim about the corpus, not about the languages. Tamil \textit{puṇarcci} genuinely
voices post-nasal stops in speech; what the corpus shows is that romanisers do not
consistently write that voicing. Whether the distribution reflects spelling convention,
orthographic interference from English, or annotator inconsistency, a transliteration model
trained to match these references inherits it.

\section{Target-side disagreement}
\label{sec:disagree}

If the residual uncertainty were source-side script ambiguity, phonological supervision would
be well targeted. Table~\ref{tab:disagree} shows it is not. Restricting attention to native
words with two or more distinct attested romanisations---19{,}812 Tamil words and 16{,}473
Malayalam---we ask, for each plosive slot, whether the romanisations of the \emph{same} word
agree about voicing.

They frequently do not, and the disagreement is concentrated exactly where the phonology is
strongest: $65.4\%$ of Tamil post-nasal slots and $36.9\%$ of Malayalam are spelled both ways.
By contrast, geminates---where voicelessness is categorical---disagree on $2.1\%$ and $0.2\%$
of slots. The contexts in which the grammar most clearly predicts voicing are the contexts in
which writers least agree on whether to write it.

A single word illustrates the pattern. Tamil \textit{koṇṭāṭi} appears in Dakshina as
\textit{kondaadi}, \textit{kondaati}, \textit{kontaadi} and \textit{kontaati}---four
romanisations of one word, splitting two-against-two on the voicing of the post-nasal
\textit{ka}. There is no fact about the source string that adjudicates between them. An
oracle with perfect knowledge of Tamil phonotactics would still be wrong roughly half the
time on this word, because the reference itself is not a function of the input.

This bounds what any source-side intervention can achieve. Phonological supervision can help
a model learn the majority mapping faster; it cannot make a multi-modal target
single-modal.

\section{Does phonological bias help a transliteration model?}
\label{sec:scale}

\paragraph{Arms.} We compare a character-level baseline (\textsc{Base}) against two distinct
implementations of phonological inductive bias:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Auxiliary decoder loss.} A linear head on the decoder output predicts the
phonotactic context class of each target akshara, trained jointly with the transduction
objective at weight $\lambda=0.3$. Labels are derived from the native-script target.
\item \textbf{Phonotactic tags in the output string.} Each target plosive is prefixed with a
private-use codepoint naming its context, so the decoder must emit context explicitly. The
\emph{source} string is untouched, so no information leaks at test time; tags are stripped
from both prediction and reference before scoring.
\end{itemize}

Both are compared against a baseline trained on identical data with an identical architecture.
At 25k pairs, for instance, the baseline has $11{,}093{,}043$ parameters and the
auxiliary-loss arm $11{,}095{,}608$---a difference of $2{,}565$, entirely the added head.
Test sets are the standard Dakshina splits: 11{,}499 Tamil and 12{,}451 Malayalam words,
identical across arms.

\paragraph{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scale} report
\textsc{en}$\rightarrow$\textsc{indic} exact match. We deliberately do \emph{not} draw a
single curve through all five scales, because the two mechanisms are different interventions
and pooling them would confound treatment with scale. Within each mechanism the pattern is
the same:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Auxiliary loss.} At 25k pairs, phonological supervision gains $+0.94$ points on
Tamil ($p=0.098$) and $+1.26$ on Malayalam ($p=0.009$). At 1.0M pairs in a joint bilingual
model, the same intervention \emph{loses} $-2.33$ on Tamil ($p=0.0003$) and $-1.37$ on
Malayalam ($p=0.030$).
\item \textbf{Output-string tags.} At 250k pairs with a recurrent encoder--decoder, tags gain
$+1.53$ on Tamil ($p=0.018$) and $+1.08$ on Malayalam ($p=0.089$). The same intervention on a
Transformer at 250k gives $+0.77$ and $-0.10$, and at 500k gives $-0.22$ and $-0.54$, none
significant.
\end{itemize}

That the sign flip replicates under two mechanisms that share nothing but their linguistic
motivation is the strongest evidence we have that it is a property of the problem rather than
of an implementation. It is also what argmax invariance predicts. In the low-data regime the
model has not yet learned the majority mapping, and being told the context is a useful
shortcut. Once enough data has been seen to recover that mapping directly, the auxiliary
objective competes with the primary one for capacity and gradient, and the tags lengthen the
target sequence, while the information they add is information the model already has.

\section{Downstream effect}
\label{sec:downstream}

A transliteration gain that does not survive into a downstream task is of limited interest,
so we test both schemes as a preprocessing step for code-mixed sentiment classification on
\textsc{Theedhum Nandrum} \cite{lakshmanan2020theedhum,chakravarthi-etal-2020-corpus}, under
two setups: replacing the romanised input with its native-script transliteration, and
concatenating the two.

Table~\ref{tab:downstream} reports the outcome, and the honest reading is that nothing
happens. Every gap is under $1.4$ macro-F1 points on a test set of roughly $4.5$k examples,
and the two setups disagree in sign on Malayalam: the baseline transliteration wins under
replacement while the phonology-aware one wins under augmentation. We report both rather than
the more favourable one. Neither scheme reliably moves downstream sentiment, which is
consistent with \S\ref{sec:scale}: an intervention that does not change transliteration
accuracy at scale has no channel through which to change anything downstream.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Single runs, no seeding.} Every number in this paper is one training run. The
codebase does not seed \texttt{torch} or \texttt{numpy}, so we cannot report variance across
initialisations. The two results we describe as significant have $p\le0.03$ on test sets of
$\ge11$k words and replicate in direction across mechanisms, but the individual small deltas
in Table~\ref{tab:scale} should not be read as ordered.

\paragraph{Unpaired analysis of a paired design.} Both arms are evaluated on the same test
set, so a two-proportion $z$-test overstates the standard error. Our tests are therefore
conservative---the significant results would survive a paired test---but McNemar's test would
be the correct choice and requires per-example predictions we did not retain.

\paragraph{Alignment sensitivity.} The entropy figures depend on the aligner. An independent
reimplementation gives Tamil alignment $85.1\%$ (vs.\ $94.8\%$), intervocalic
$n=184{,}496$ (vs.\ $133{,}930$) and $H(V\mid C)=0.641$ (vs.\ $0.671$); see
Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline}. Both implementations agree that $P(\text{voiced})<0.5$ in every
context and that the argmax error reduction is $0.00\%$, so the paper's central claim is
robust to the aligner, but the third decimal place is not.

\paragraph{Annotation provenance.} Dakshina records carry no annotator identifier, so
\S\ref{sec:disagree} measures disagreement among \emph{romanisation variants} of a word, not
between identified annotators. Multiple variants may originate from one writer.

\paragraph{Scope.} We test one language pair direction (\textsc{en}$\rightarrow$\textsc{indic}),
one decoding strategy (greedy), and two Dravidian languages. The 3.2M-pair multilingual run
that would have extended this to eight languages was invalidated by an implementation defect
(Appendix~\ref{app:control}) and is not evidence either way.

\section{Conclusion}

Tamil and Malayalam orthography is underspecified for voicing, and that underspecification is
real: phonotactic context carries $0.14$--$0.16$ bits of information about how a plosive will
be romanised. But it is not \emph{undecidable}. The voiceless realisation is the majority
outcome in every context, so the optimal context-conditional decision rule and the optimal
unconditional one are the same rule. Phonological supervision accordingly helps only while a
model has too little data to find that rule on its own, and hurts once it has enough. The
uncertainty that remains lives in the references---annotators writing the same word both
ways two-thirds of the time in precisely the context where the grammar is clearest---and is
beyond the reach of any source-side prior.

For practitioners the implication is narrow but useful: reach for explicit phonological
structure in genuinely low-resource settings, and expect nothing from it at scale. For
benchmark builders the implication is larger. When $64\%$ of post-nasal plosives are written
both ways by competent romanisers of the same word, the question of what the reference
\emph{should} be is prior to the question of which model matches it best.

\section{Entropy pipeline divergence}
\label{app:pipeline}

Two independent implementations of the akshara aligner produce different alignment rates and
different intervocalic slot counts, and therefore different entropies to the second decimal.
The primary implementation reported in Table~\ref{tab:entropy} aligns $94.8\%$ of Tamil
words; the reimplementation aligns $85.1\%$ and, being more permissive about intervocalic
position, finds $184{,}496$ intervocalic slots against $133{,}930$. Conditional entropy is
$0.641$ against $0.671$ bits. Neither difference affects the sign of any per-context
majority or the $0.00\%$ argmax gain.

\section{The 3.2M-pair run is a negative control, not a null result}
\label{app:control}

We trained an eight-language multilingual model on 3.2M pairs with the auxiliary-loss
mechanism, intending it as the large-scale endpoint of \S\ref{sec:scale}. Post-hoc audit
showed the treatment was not administered. In that training script the loop that walks the
native-script word computes a phonotactic context for each plosive and discards it; the labels
actually used are recomputed from the romanised \emph{source} string, and the auxiliary head
is attached to the encoder, whose input is that same string. The auxiliary task was thus to
predict a deterministic function of the encoder's own input. Separately, the label function
returns early for any language outside Tamil and Malayalam, so for the other six languages
every label was the constant \textsc{none}, and the Tamil and Malayalam nasal inventories were
exchanged.

Table~\ref{tab:control} reports the run for the record. Its near-zero deltas are not evidence
that phonological bias fails at 3.2M pairs; they are what one expects when no phonological
information is supplied. We flag it explicitly rather than omitting it because the run
appears in earlier drafts of this work as the paper's headline scaling result.
